How This Playbook Works

The Claude Project chat is a very detailed, trained assistant. Type a question or a request in plain language.

  • It is trained on the business: the playbook, rules, persona, voice guide and known traps are loaded as knowledge files, so answers follow how FINTECH Circle works.
  • It is connected to the live systems: it reads Tantra, Instantly, GoHighLevel, WebinarGeek and the outreach sheet directly, so numbers are read at the source each time.
  • It answers, and it can act: most of the time it answers. When asked to do a task it shows a plan first and waits for a typed "go", or "send now" for anything that reaches people. It never acts on its own.

How to use this document. Chapters 1 to 7 follow a registrant's journey: the funnel on one page, traffic, content, landing and registration, nurture, the webinar, and sales and CRM. Chapter 8 is the cold outreach operations manual, with the tools and the traps. Chapters 9 and 10 cover the dashboard, where every document lives, and security. Chapter 11 lists the open decisions and next actions. Live numbers (RSVPs, revenue, lead counts, campaign status) are never written here. Each one is read from the source each time, using the dashboard link in 9.1 or the chat.

1. The Funnel on One Page

Six stages carry a stranger to a paying client: traffic, landing and registration, nurture, the webinar, the sales call, and the CRM. Several traffic channels run side by side on purpose, because one channel alone is a risk. The webinar is the conversion mechanism. Everything else exists to fill it and to follow up on it.

1.1. What the Funnel Is For

FINTECH Circle runs a free live webinar for senior financial-services leaders who want board, non-executive, advisory or fractional roles in UK fintech. The webinar leads to an application, a call and, for those accepted, enrolment in the Board Programme.

  • Webinar headline in use: The Insider Roadmap to Access Top UK Fintech Board Seats in 2026
  • Who it is for ("UK Portfolio Finance Leader"): 15 to 30+ years in banking, insurance, asset management, fintech or top-tier consulting and audit. Current or former C-suite, board member, NED or partner, moving away from a full-time role.
  • Wants: paid part-time strategic work, a meaningful title, to give back, intellectual engagement.
  • Fears: regulatory and reputational risk, and paying for courses that do not lead to roles.
  • Core problem: UK board seats are mostly filled through networks and headhunter shortlists before they are advertised.
  • Who is at the centre: Susanne Chishti, Founder & Chair of FINTECH Circle, nearly three decades in financial services, FTSE board member, co-editor of the FINTECH Book and its five companion titles. Her voice and experience are the source of the content.

1.5. How a Request Runs, and the Three Safety Levels

  1. The team types the request in the chat.
  2. Claude reads the knowledge files (the rules and traps) and calls the connector tools (live data).
  3. Claude shows a plan card: what was found now, what it will change, what it will not touch, risks, rollback, how it will verify.
  4. The team replies "go" (level 2) or "send now" (level 3).
  5. Claude reports each figure from its own source, in the turn it read it.
Level Typed word What it covers
Read none Numbers, lists, status, previews. Changes nothing.
Change "go" Creating a draft, a tab, a tag, a broadcast, a template; editing a draft or paused campaign; blocking a domain.
Reaches people "send now" Sending invites, activating a campaign, enrolling contacts in a live workflow, registering a person, cancelling a wave, editing an active campaign. Irreversible, so never assumed.

Truth rules. (1) A claim about a live system is valid only in the turn it was read. (2) Vendor counters are not proof of delivery; outcomes are confirmed at the system that did the work. (3) Procedure lives in code, not in reminders; anything that says "remember to" is enforced by a tool instead.

2. Traffic

2.1. The Order of Channels

A new funnel starts with cold outreach, because a few hundred invites validate the funnel quickly. Content starts alongside it. Once the content shows what the audience responds to, the best posts are promoted with paid ads. The long-term direction is to make organic content the largest share of traffic.

ChannelBest forCost of running it
Organic content (LinkedIn)Reputation, steady registrations, learning what resonatesTime and quality control. No media spend.
Paid ads (LinkedIn)Scaling the posts that already workMedia spend. Needs proven creative first.
Cold calendar invites (Tantra)Fast traffic before an eventWarmed mailboxes, fresh data, domain upkeep
Cold email (Instantly)Fast traffic, A/B testing of anglesDomain rotation, deliverability monitoring

Outreach is the quick fix. The infrastructure is hard to maintain and deliverability gets worse over time. That is why the direction is toward content and ads.

2.2. Organic Content First, Then Boost

The workflow is two steps. Publish organic posts and read the results after a couple of weeks. Then boost the posts that performed, using LinkedIn ads. A post published from a personal profile can now be boosted through the company page, which was not possible before. Plan the boost into the content calendar instead of writing ad creative from scratch.

Creative decides whether an ad works, and the organic posts are the cheapest way to find the creative that does. Content production is in chapter 3.

2.3. Cold Outreach as the Fast Channel

Calendar invites (Tantra) and cold email (Instantly) both point to the registration page. Chapter 8 is the full operating manual. Three points matter at the planning level:

  • Outreach brings traffic quickly but needs constant upkeep: warm-up, domain rotation and list quality.
  • Reused data loses deliverability over time, and that can damage the sending domains for both channels.
  • Results are judged at day 3 to 4, never day 1.

2.4. Buying New Cold Data

STATUS: DECISION AGREED ON THE 14 OCT CALL, NOT YET ACTIONED AS OF: 2026-10-14

The decision on the call was to stop buying new cold names and to work with the data already owned. The cost is deliverability: the longer the same pool is reused, the more bounces and the more risk to the sending domains. If cold calendar invites remain worth keeping, fresh data in small amounts is the safer practice. How much of the owned pool is still reusable is read live (see 8.7), not written here.

3. The Content Engine

3.1. The Audience Bar

The audience is board level and the most discerning one FINTECH Circle can reach. Two failures do the damage: anything that reads as AI slop, and statements so generic that anyone could have written them. Either one costs credibility that took years to build.

Susanne reviews every post until the quality is proven. Only then is more of the process automated. Her own experience is the raw material. The system produces and checks. It does not invent.

3.2. The Content Workflow

Susanne is the source; the team produces; Susanne approves.

  1. Brief (the team): format, topic or Susanne's notes, source material, call to action (webinar or follow), date.
  2. Claude drafts: post copy in her voice, first-comment text, slide-by-slide text with a layout note, a design brief naming the template.
  3. Claude checks against the approval checklist and fixes any failure.
  4. Susanne signs off every post. Recorded in the Content Calendar tab of the Work Tracker.
  5. Design and schedule (the team): finished in the named template, scheduled in Metricool or LinkedIn.

Ten approved formats. Image: Experiential · Thought leadership · Quotes · Cheatsheet. Carousel: Framework deck · Comparison deck · Checklist deck · Illustrated story · Book or resource · Case study (real, permissioned cases only). Videos are out of scope for now.

What goes in (inputs).

  • Her voice: her tone-of-voice questionnaire (friendly, knowledgeable, positive; nothing negative or controversial); first person, authority lived and never claimed; structure of hook, tension, three numbered points, stakes, one call to action; her approved posts as the quality bar; British English.
  • Substance: her career and boards, the FINTECH Book series, the Board Programme and its nine modules, the webinar headline, testimonials only with permission.
  • Audience: the persona in 1.1.
  • Design: brand palette (ocean, navy, dusk, sky, sun), the identity chip, page numbers and footer logo on every slide, the craft rules, the named template per format, finished example carousels as structure references only.
  • CTA rules: webinar posts put the registration link in the first comment and end with "Link in the first comment." Follow posts end with the follow line. One call to action per post and the post ends on it.

Never: hype words, manufactured urgency, "comment and I'll DM", a closing question to the reader, invented statistics, clients or quotes, anything negative or critical, anything market-sensitive or confidential, investment advice, another client's content, the long dash (use a short hyphen), and the name of the listed bank she sits on.

Weekly routine: the team sends the brief → Claude returns the draft, already checked → Susanne approves or edits → the team designs and schedules → log it in Content Calendar. Target volume: [__ posts per week - to confirm].

3.4. The Weekly Loop

The structure below comes from the Content Engine Playbook. It is the pattern for running a batch once the tools are in place. Use it as the target shape, adapted to the ten formats and the sign-off rule in 3.2.

  1. One context bucket. A single set of files holds the company and offer, positioning, Susanne's voice, verified facts with sources, brand kit, the webinar deck, cleared case studies, a registry of used content, and the format taxonomy.
  2. Ideas first, one human gate. Each week the agent proposes ideas drawn from the bucket. A person chooses which to build. That is the single approval pause before copy is written.
  3. Match each idea to a strong format before writing, so design is never an afterthought.
  4. Copy under fixed rules: every claim has a source, nothing is written for an insider, no repeated angles (check the registry).
  5. Build, render, critique. Render the design, check it at phone size, fix, repeat until it matches the quality of the best example.
  6. Review, schedule, read the results. Susanne signs off, the team schedules in Metricool, and the results feed the next week's ideas.

Rules carried over from that playbook and already in force here: no claim without a source, no invented statistics, real photos, clients named only with written clearance, links in the first comment and not in the post body.

3.5. Measuring Content

4. Landing, Registration and Tracking

4.1. Check the Landing Page and the Automations

Before the invites land, confirm the registration page shows the new date and that the registration path works end to end.

  • The page reads the upcoming broadcast from the backend; verify the date shown.
  • Test one registration: a confirmation email arrives from WebinarGeek, and (if a phone number is given) the WhatsApp join-link and reminder path starts.
  • Timezone must agree in two places: the page and the Worker both use Europe/London. Nothing enforces this, so check it when the schedule changes.
  • Landing page changes: Claude writes a precise change request (page, section, old text to new text, and why). The operations team applies it, previews it, and publishes only after a test registration and a "go".

4.2. What Happens When Someone Registers (Automatic)

  1. The registration form sends one submit to four places at once: WebinarGeek (the only one that can fail the registration), a Google Sheet, GoHighLevel, and the thank-you page.
  2. WebinarGeek sends the confirmation immediately and the reminders on schedule.
  3. The thank-you page asks the registrant to WhatsApp "send me my join link". That inbound message opens WhatsApp's 24-hour window, which is why the confirmation can be a free-form message. This ask is load-bearing; do not remove it.
  4. GoHighLevel replies with the join link, then sends four timed WhatsApp reminders anchored on the broadcast date. The morning reminder sits 4.5 hours before the broadcast.
  5. The Worker keeps GHL up to date from WebinarGeek events (registered, unsubscribed, watched live, watched replay, no-show) by setting state tags.
  6. A registrant without a phone number still gets the full WebinarGeek email journey; they just do not get WhatsApp.

4.3. Website Tracking

7. Sales and CRM (GoHighLevel)

7.1. The Pipeline and the Conversation View

GHL is the CRM of record: every registrant is a contact, and it is the source of truth for contact data. It offers more than is used today.

  • Pipelines as Kanban boards. A sales pipeline tracks each applicant from application to call to payment as a card. Other pipelines can track delivery or course curriculum.
  • One conversation view per contact. Email, SMS and WhatsApp messages sit in the same thread beside the contact's activity: forms submitted, pages visited (once 4.3 is done), tags.
  • Roles when volume grows. One person sets appointments or reviews applications, another takes the calls.

Today: the Opportunities pipeline was empty when last read, and revenue comes from payment transactions. Whether to start using the pipeline is an open decision (11.1).

8.2. Send the Calendar Invites (Tantra)

What happens. Tantra creates a Google Calendar event and adds the leads as attendees from 20 warmed mailboxes. Creating a calendar campaign is the send. There is no draft and no pause. Type: "Plan the next wave for the upcoming webinar."

  1. Claude selects leads: unused only, never stamped on any tab, never contacted in Tantra or Instantly, minus RSVPs, attendees, suppressed people, blocked domains and excluded countries and titles.
  2. Claude shows the plan card: counts per wave, the invite variation per wave, the schedule, the geo mix and why leads were skipped.
  3. On "send now", Claude creates each campaign with the required settings, confirms each reads ready, then marks only those leads as used.
  4. If any wave is not ready, Claude re-checks a few minutes later. Delivery truth comes from the calendar RSVPs and the next day's dashboard.

Settings enforced on every wave: balanced distribution across mailboxes; London time for the event; about 1,000 invites per campaign; WebinarGeek broadcast linked and yes/maybe auto-registered; invite location is always the registration link with its tracking tag, never the WebinarGeek page; one variationN tag; no first-name placeholders in titles; stale dates blocked. The five invite titles (V1 to V5) are fixed and never renumbered; their results are tracked and used to pick the next wave's mix. Fixing a mistake: wave_cancel removes a wave. It first says whether the invites are still scheduled (cancelling should stop them) or already delivered (cancelling cannot recall them). It needs "send now", and it leaves the leads stamped as used.

8.3. Launch the Cold Email (Instantly)

Type: "Set up the Instantly campaign for the next webinar."

  1. Claude lists campaigns (ids change every webinar) and reads the latest one.
  2. It proposes the date and time replacements as a plan card.
  3. On "go" it copies the campaign into a new draft; it refuses if any old date or time is left in the copy.
  4. It previews the leads, with suppressed, blocked and previously contacted people removed, then loads them in batches on "go".
  5. The team reviews the draft together. Claude can edit any step (subject, body, delay), the name, daily limit or end date; it shows the exact before and after and blocks stale dates.
  6. The campaign starts only on "send now".

Current pattern: two cold variants A/B-testing a Personal and a Value angle, plus a retarget campaign aimed at the previous webinar's yes/maybe no-shows. Trap: a campaign name can say "DRAFT" while its status is active. Trust the status field, never the name.

8.4. Review and Learn

  • Read RSVPs from the calendar (not Tantra's sent counter, which reads zero for calendar campaigns that did send).
  • Compare cold-email performance, bounce rates and revenue by tier.
  • Feed what worked (invite titles, countries, seniority) into the next wave's mix. Weak or high-bounce lists are not reused.

8.5. Protect the List at Any Time

Type: "This company asked to be removed. Block them everywhere." Claude blocks the whole domain in Instantly, marks every record at that domain as unsubscribed in Tantra (contacts are duplicated per list, so every record is patched), and adds it to the shared suppression list. Every later lead preview then excludes them. It never blocks a free email provider or one of our own sending domains. Blocking is not erasure: a deletion request also needs the row removed from the sheets and a reply sent.

Before every wave, run the sweep: python3 ../../_method/scripts/reply_sweep.py (dry run), then add --apply on "go".

8.6. Calendar and Cold-Email Rules (Breaking These Breaks Sends)

  • Calendar invites: balanced distribution, never fill-first; event time in London; about 1,000 per campaign; broadcast linked; auto-register on; location is the registration link; send 3 to 7 days before. Capacity is limited by the shared Google quota, about 1,500 invites per service account per day.
  • Creating a calendar campaign is the send. Only name and tags can be edited afterwards; to change anything else, cancel and recreate.
  • Cold email: one campaign per webinar named Webinar <n> - <D Month>; stays a draft until "send now"; ids change every webinar so always list.
  • A decline of a calendar invite is treated as an opt-out.

8.7. Outreach Inputs (What Claude Uses to Target and Write)

  • Who: the persona; the nine financial industries; country and title filters; exclusions for attendees, RSVPs, applicants, customers, suppressed people, blocked domains and other clients; anyone already invited on any tab or contacted in Tantra or Instantly.
  • When: webinar title, date and time in London; the WebinarGeek broadcast; the registration link; 3 to 7 days before the event.
  • What we say: the five invite titles and body; cold-email steps in two angles; a retarget sequence.
  • How we send: balanced across mailboxes; leads stamped only after the campaign reads ready.
  • What worked: yes rates by invite title and segment, bounce rates, best countries and seniority.

8.8. Sender Mailboxes, Domains and Deliverability

  • Mailboxes. Tantra sends from a pool of warmed Google Workspace mailboxes, and their calendars record Yes, Maybe and No. Instantly sends from a separate set of cold-email mailboxes.
  • Domains and inboxes. Domains are registered in Namecheap. The cold-email inboxes come from a supplier and connect to Instantly. DNS sits in Cloudflare.
  • Health check. A weekly job checks the settings on every domain that sends mail and reports to the dashboard. Domains that send through a third-party mail service can show a warning for the missing Google SPF record. That is expected for them, and the dashboard should label them as such.
  • Bounce rate is the signal to watch, not the raw bounce count. Verify a list before reusing the domains that sent it.
  • Calendar-invite replies land in the sender mailboxes and are not yet read by anyone (11.2).
  • New domains or senders are infrastructure: run python3 ../../_method/scripts/preflight_domain.py <name> first, then show a plan card.

8.12. Known Traps (Where Wrong Answers Come From)

  • Tantra under-reports outreach (deleted campaigns are invisible) and its sentCount is 0 for calendar campaigns. Use the calendar RSVPs.
  • Instantly analytics under-report replies badly and show 0 opens (tracking is off). Do not quote them as real.
  • Master sheets for early webinars are lead lists with an empty tracking scaffold: they show who was targeted, never what was sent.
  • Early "applications" and "paid customers" files are stale exports. Revenue comes live from GHL payments.
  • Some raw lists store first and last names swapped; cross-check against the email before trusting a first name.
  • A calendar campaign created without the broadcast link can never be linked afterwards (the field is create-only).
  • Never judge a wave on day 1; RSVPs accrue.

10. Security and Backups

10.1. Risks and Safeguards

  • The main risk when AI builds software is a leaked key, typically committed to a public repository. Claude is now reliable at not doing this, but the safeguards below do not rely on that.
  • Keys stay in each system's own secret store (for example the Worker's secrets, or a local .env that is never committed). They are never printed, pasted in chat or stored in a document.
  • A pre-commit secret scan and a .gitignore block keys, service-account files, lead lists, suppression exports and anything with personal data.
  • Worst realistic outcome: a few days of lost promotion, or some API credit used. Moving to a no-AI tool for pages would remove that risk but is not recommended, because a broken page can still be seen and fixed.
  • Public pages. The dashboard has no login. Anyone with the link sees revenue and customer figures. Putting Cloudflare Access in front of it is an open decision (11.1).
